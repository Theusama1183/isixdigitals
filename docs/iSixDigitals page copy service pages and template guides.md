## 1. /studios/dance

**Page type:** Service · **Schema:** Service · **Canonical path:** `/studios/dance`

**Title tag (full, brand included, 54 characters):** Dance Studio Website Design for Schools | iSixDigitals

**Meta description (147 characters):** Website design for dance studios and schools: timetables that work on a phone, clear trial class booking and separate paths for parents and adults.

**H1 (54 characters):** Dance studio website design built around the timetable

### Body copy

This page is for dance studio owners and school principals who need a website that helps a new student choose a class and book it. Most dance studio websites go wrong in the same three places: the timetable, the levels and the first class. Below is what a dance studio website has to do, which pages it needs, and how I handle each of those problems.

#### What a dance studio website has to do

A dance studio website has five jobs. If it does these well, most of the rest follows.

1. **Help a new student find the right class and level.** Someone arriving cold does not know whether they are Beginner, Grade 2 or Intermediate. Every class needs a plain description of who it is for, what happens in the room and what experience is expected.
2. **Make the timetable legible on a phone.** Timetables get checked on phones, often in a hurry. If the timetable only works on a desktop screen, it is not working.
3. **Explain what a trial class involves.** First-time students and parents want to know what to wear, how long it lasts, whether they can watch and what happens afterwards. Answer those before they have to ask.
4. **Separate adult classes, children’s classes and vocational training.** These audiences have different questions, different schedules and different reasons to choose you. One mixed list makes all three harder to read.
5. **Give parents and adult students different paths.** A parent is looking for an age group, a safe environment and a time that fits around school. An adult student is looking for a level, an evening slot and a low-pressure first visit. One route through the site cannot serve both well.

#### The pages a dance studio actually needs

Website design for dance studios is not the generic home, about, services and contact set. These are the pages that carry the weight:

- **Home:** who the studio is for, the class types on offer, and direct routes to the timetable and to booking a first class.
- **Timetable and classes:** every class by day, age group and level, filterable, and readable on a phone.
- **Class detail:** one page per class or class family, covering level, age range, what to wear, the teacher and the booking route.
- **About and teachers:** who teaches, their training and the studio’s approach, written for a nervous parent as much as a keen dancer.
- **Fees:** what a trial class, a drop-in and a term cost, stated plainly, or a clear way to ask when fees vary.
- **Trial class booking:** a short form built for first-time students only.
- **Contact:** location, access and parking, and a way to ask a general question.

#### Timetables, levels and the mobile problem

Three failures turn up again and again on dance studio websites.

**The PDF timetable.** A PDF has to be downloaded, pinched and zoomed. It cannot be filtered by age or level, it cannot link to a class page, and it goes out of date the moment a class changes. A visitor on a phone will often give up before finding their class.

**The grid that breaks on a phone.** A seven-column week grid works on a large screen. On a phone it either shrinks until it cannot be read or scrolls sideways and hides most of the week. The fix is a different layout, not a smaller grid. On small screens, show the timetable one day at a time or as a list grouped by day, with filters for age group and level, and let each class open its own detail page.

**Levels with no explanation.** “Level 2” means something different in every studio. Describe each level by what a dancer can already do, and say how a student moves up. A newcomer should be able to place themselves without phoning the front desk.

Behind all three sits one rule: the timetable lives in one place and is shown wherever it is needed. If you are weighing a template against a custom build, this guide to what dance studio website templates get wrong covers the same problem from the buyer’s side.

#### Trial classes and enquiries

A dance studio receives three different kinds of enquiry, and each needs its own handling.

- **A trial booking** needs a class, a date, the student’s age and any experience. It should take a minute.
- **A term registration** needs fuller details: contact information, emergency contacts, medical notes and consent, usually collected through the registration tool the studio already uses.
- **A general question** needs a message box and nothing else.

One form cannot serve all three. A trial form that asks for medical and emergency details puts people off at the moment they are least committed. A single contact form used for bookings leaves the studio chasing missing details by email.

The better structure is separate routes. A “book a trial class” button sits on every class page. Registration links go to the right tool for that class, with the class already chosen. A short contact form handles everything else.

## 2. /studios/film

**H1 (63 characters):** A videographer portfolio website built to win project enquiries

### Body copy

You make good work and your website does not show it. Clients find you through Instagram or Vimeo, send a message, and the conversation starts without the information either side needs. This page is for videographers, filmmakers, photographers and small production companies. It covers how to structure a videographer portfolio website, how to keep reels fast, and how to turn a visit into a project enquiry.

#### Why a reel alone does not win the project

A reel answers one question: can this person make good work? The person watching it is asking several more. Do you handle projects like theirs? Is your work in the right range for their budget? Are you free when they need you? Can you deliver the formats they require?

A site that shows only a reel leaves those questions to a back-and-forth by message. A site that answers them in advance tends to get enquiries that arrive with more of the detail you need, and fewer good prospects go quiet.

#### How to structure a videographer portfolio

Start with how a client searches, not how you filed your hard drive. Organise projects by the work clients are looking for. There are three common ways to group them, and each has a cost.

- **By client type** (weddings, corporate, charity, hospitality). This matches how buyers think. The cost is deciding where work that fits two groups should live.
- **By format** (documentary, music video, brand film, social cut). This suits people who already know what they want to commission. Many buyers do not know the format name for what they need.
- **By outcome** (a product launch, a fundraising appeal, an event recap). This is persuasive, but every project needs enough context to show the outcome, and that takes more writing.

For most videographers, client type is the safest primary grouping, with format as a label on each project. Whatever you choose, show fewer pieces. Twelve strong projects serve a visitor better than sixty, because every weak piece lowers the impression of the strong ones.

Each project page needs four things: the brief in a sentence or two, what you delivered, your credit and role, and the runtime. That is enough for a producer to judge fit without a call. The typical set of pages is home, a work index, project pages, about and contact.

For public examples of how other videographers group and present their work, see videographer website examples.

#### Reels and video that behave on a phone

Video-led sites are heavy by nature, so the decisions that matter are decisions about restraint.

**Autoplay and sound.** A short, muted loop at the top of the page is fine on a desktop. Do not autoplay anything with sound. Phones and data-saving modes often block autoplay, so the page has to look finished without it.

**Poster frames.** The still image shown before a video plays is the first impression on mobile. Choose a frame that says what the project is, not a blurred mid-motion frame.

**File weight.** One embedded player is light. A work index with twenty players loading at once is not. Load a poster image first and the player only when someone taps it.

**What fast means.** For a video site, fast means the visitor can see work and use the navigation quickly. It does not mean the video file is small. Reels and image galleries must stay fast and accessible, which includes captions or transcripts where the work depends on dialogue, and controls that work from a keyboard.

**Runtime.** On a landing page, a 90-second showreel does more than a six-minute one. Visitors give a page seconds, not minutes. Keep the longer cut on its own page for people who want to keep watching.

Photographers have the same problem with images: serve sizes that suit the screen, load images below the fold as the visitor scrolls, and write real alt text.

#### Turning a portfolio into an enquiry

Project enquiries need a clear place to begin. A production client needs three things before they write to you.

1. **Availability.** A line saying whether you are taking projects, and roughly when.
2. **Typical project shape.** What you usually make, the size of team you bring and the deliverables that come with it.
3. **A clear next step.** One button, one form, and a sentence saying what happens after they send it.

One form beats a contact page full of email addresses and social links because it can ask the questions you would ask anyway: the type of project, the timeline, the location, the deliverables and, if you want it, a budget range. You can then reply with something useful instead of opening with questions.

#### Film, video and photography: one page or three?

These kinds of work attract different clients and need different emphasis. Photographers need image-first galleries and licensing language. Videographers need reels, runtime and credits. Production companies need capabilities, past credits and a way to scope a larger project.

If one practice does several of these, the home page has to know which visitor it is talking to, and the structure follows from which work leads. One undifferentiated page tends to serve none of them well. For a look at how photographers handle this, see photography website examples.

**Question:** Can you work with the reels we already have on Vimeo?

**Answer:** Yes. Reels hosted on Vimeo or YouTube can be embedded in the site, so there is no need to upload them again. I set how they load so the page stays fast.

**Answer:** Images are resized and compressed into several versions so each device downloads only what it needs. Video is usually best left with a video host, with the site loading the player when a visitor chooses to watch.

## 3. /studios/music-art

**Page type:** Service · **Schema:** Service · **Canonical path:** `/studios/music-art`

**Title tag (full, brand included, 58 characters):** Website Design for Artists and Music Spaces | iSixDigitals

**Meta description (148 characters):** Website design for artists, recording studios and art spaces: portfolios that organise a body of work, and booking paths for sessions and workshops.

**H1 (65 characters):** Website design for artists, recording studios and creative spaces

### Body copy

This page is for two kinds of reader. The first is an independent artist, such as a painter, ceramicist, illustrator or musician, who needs a portfolio and a clear way to be contacted. The second runs a recording studio, rehearsal room or art space and needs to present a physical space and take bookings. Both need a site that explains the work and makes the next step obvious.

#### What an artist’s website is for

An artist’s website is not a gallery mirror. It is where a curator, a commissioner, a gallery, a journalist or a customer decides whether to get in touch. That reframes the design. The job is not to show everything you have made. It is to let a stranger understand your work quickly, see that you are active, and know how to reach you for the thing they want.

Good artist website design starts from who is visiting and what they are deciding. The navigation, the caption style and the page order all follow from that.

#### The portfolio problem

A large, changing or partly unsold body of work needs decisions made on purpose.

- **Series or singles.** Group by series when works belong together and gain from being seen together. Show singles when each piece stands alone. Mixing both without labels leaves a visitor unsure what they are looking at.
- **Chronological or thematic.** Chronological suits an archive and shows development. Thematic suits a visitor who is deciding quickly. A common answer is a thematic front, with the archive one click behind.
- **Captions.** Give medium, dimensions and year for every work. Add status (available, sold, in a collection) only if you will keep it accurate.
- **How much text.** A line or two per work, and a longer statement for each series where the thinking matters. Long text beside every image is rarely read.

Work that is not for sale still belongs on the site. Mark it clearly so no one writes to ask the price. To see how other artists organise and caption their sites, read these artist website examples worth studying.

#### Commissions, sales and enquiries

An artist usually receives three kinds of enquiry: a commission, an exhibition or representation approach, and a purchase. They need different paths because they need different information.

- A **commission** enquiry needs the brief, size, timeline and location.
- An **exhibition or representation** enquiry needs your statement, CV and links to recent work.
- A **purchase** enquiry needs availability, price and shipping.

One contact form asks none of these well. A short selector at the start of the enquiry works better: “I’d like to commission a work”, “I’m a curator or gallery”, “I’d like to buy a work”, “Something else”. Each choice leads to the right questions.

A shop makes sense when you have works or editions at fixed prices and can pack and ship them reliably. It is the wrong answer when every piece is unique and individually priced, when your practice is mostly commissions, or when stock is thin. An empty or half-empty shop looks worse than an enquiry route with images and availability.

#### For recording, rehearsal and art spaces

If you run a space rather than a practice, the site has a different job: it lets a stranger judge a room before they visit. Show the space, equipment and services that matter, and leave out the rest. A bookable space needs:

- **Rooms and specs:** size, the live room and control room, and what each room suits.
- **Equipment:** what is in the room and what can be brought, limited to what a booker cares about.
- **Rates or a quote path:** published rates if they are stable, or a “request a quote” route that asks the right questions.
- **Availability:** how people find out when rooms are free, and how far ahead to ask.
- **House rules:** arrival and load-in, cancellations, and what is and is not allowed.

Separate session enquiries from workshops and collaborations. A band booking a day and a musician proposing a collaboration are different people with different questions. Each route should explain preparation, availability and the next step clearly.

If you are considering a ready-made theme first, this guide explains why music studio templates struggle with bookings. It applies to any music studio website template, not only to one.

#### Images, video and the weight problem

Artists and studios upload large files. A 12 MB JPEG straight from a camera loads slowly on a phone, and a visitor can leave before the work appears. The fix is routine. Compress images, create several sizes for different screens, and load images lower on the page as the visitor scrolls.

Preparation before launch prevents most delays:

- consistent photography of each work, square-on, in even light, against a neutral background
- final crops decided
- captions drafted
- file names that describe the work
- short video clips exported at a sensible size

#### Questions artists ask

**Question:** How do I start an artist website?

**Answer:** Make three decisions first: who you want to reach, which selection of your strongest work represents you, and what you want a visitor to do. Then gather images and captions before design begins. A site built from finished material goes much faster than one built around gaps.

**Question:** What should an artist website include?

**Answer:** Selected work with captions, a short statement about you and the work, a CV or exhibition list, and a clear way to make contact. Add commission information or a shop only if they match how you actually work.

**Answer:** If each piece is unique or you work mainly on commission, an enquiry route usually serves you better than a shop. A shop makes sense when you have works or editions at fixed prices and can pack and ship them reliably.

## 4. /notes/photography-website-templates

**Page type:** Editorial · **Schema:** Article · **Canonical path:** `/notes/photography-website-templates`

**Title tag (full, brand included, 58 characters):** Photography Website Templates: Honest Guide | iSixDigitals

**Meta description (140 characters):** What photography website templates give you, where they break down, and a ten-minute test for judging one before you spend your money on it.

**H1 (64 characters):** Photography website templates: what you get and what you give up

### Body copy

A template is the fastest way to get a photography website live. It is also the choice that most often disappoints once the first enquiries arrive, because the template was designed to look good in a demo, not to handle a wedding date, a licensing question or a thirty-image gallery on a phone.

This guide treats photography website templates as a decision. It covers what you actually get, where templates break down, how to judge one in ten minutes, and when to choose something else. It does not rank templates. Lists of templates date quickly, and the question that matters is whether the structure suits your work.

#### What a photography template actually gives you

A template gives you a finished layout on a platform that hosts it, usually with a handful of colour and font options. For a photographer who needs to be online soon, that has real value.

**What you get:**

- **Speed.** You can have something live much sooner than with a custom build, because the structure already exists.
- **A lower starting cost.** There is no design stage to pay for, and updates to the underlying software come from the provider.
- **A tested layout.** Responsive behaviour, basic accessibility and browser quirks have mostly been handled by someone else.

**What you give up:**

- **Distinctiveness.** Other photographers can pick the same template. Clients who browse several sites in a week notice it.
- **Control over structure.** The template decides how many levels of navigation you have, how galleries are arranged and what the contact page looks like. Those decisions were made for a generic photographer.
- **Enquiry logic.** Most templates ship a contact form with a name, an email address and a message box. That is rarely enough for a booking.

What you are buying is a layout, not a plan for how a visitor becomes an enquiry. If your work is simple and your enquiries are simple, a layout may be all you need. If not, the gaps show up quickly.

#### Where templates break down

##### Tall galleries on mobile

Many photography templates stack images in one long column on a phone. A gallery of forty images becomes forty screens of scrolling, often with every image loading at once. Visitors stop before the end, and the best work is usually not at the top. Better handling groups images into smaller sets, loads them as the visitor scrolls and offers thumbnails that open a larger view.

##### No project context

A grid of images does not say what the shoot was, who it was for, where it took place or how the pictures were used. A wedding client wants to see a whole day. A commercial client wants to see a brief and the deliverables. An editorial client wants to see where the work appeared. Template galleries are built to hold images, not projects, so the context has nowhere to go.

##### No booking path

A photographer needs different information depending on the job. A wedding needs a date, a venue and a rough guest count. A commercial job needs usage, licence length and a deadline. A portrait session needs a session type and a location. A name, an email address and a message box produces a first reply that is a list of questions, and some visitors will not wait for it.

##### Demo images flatter the layout

Template demos use images chosen and cropped to suit the design. Your own set will include portrait and landscape frames, a few weaker pictures and some awkward crops. A template that looks balanced with the demo content can look uneven with yours. That is why the test below starts by putting your own images in.

#### How to judge a template in ten minutes

You can learn most of what you need before committing. Run these checks in order, on a phone where you can.

1. **Open the demo on your phone, not on the desktop preview.** Scroll the main gallery and the work index. Note where it becomes tiring.
2. **Put your own images in.** Use a mix of portrait and landscape frames, including your weakest crops. See how the layout copes.
3. **Try a gallery of forty images and one of twelve.** Check that neither feels wrong, and that a short gallery does not leave a large empty space.
4. **Count the taps from a gallery to a message.** If a visitor who likes a picture has to hunt for the contact page, the enquiry path is weak.
5. **Look at the form.** Can you add fields for date, location, project type or usage? If the form cannot change, your enquiries will arrive without the detail you need.
6. **Check page weight on a slow connection.** Use a free speed test and a throttled mobile setting. Pay attention to how long it takes before you can see an image and use the menu.
7. **Check alt text and keyboard use.** You should be able to add a description to each image, and you should be able to move through the navigation without a mouse.
8. **Find a place for licensing and usage language.** Commercial and editorial photographers need somewhere to say how work can be used. If there is no natural place, you will end up bolting it on.
9. **Check your exit.** Find out whether you can export your images and text if you leave the platform. Moving later is easier when you can take your content with you.
10. **Write real copy for each block.** If you would have to pad the layout with filler to make it look full, the structure does not match what you have to say.

A template that passes most of these is a reasonable choice. One that fails three or four is probably costing you enquiries.

#### Template, builder or custom build

There are three routes. A template is a ready-made theme on a platform. A builder is a platform where you assemble your own pages with design tools. A custom build is a site designed around your work and your enquiries.

|  | Template | Builder | Custom build |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speed to launch | Fastest | Fast | Slowest |
| Control over structure | Low | Medium | Full |
| Enquiry logic | The form as supplied | Depends on the platform’s form tools | Designed around your work |
| Looks distinct from others | Rarely | Sometimes | Yes |
| Editing it yourself | Easy within limits | Easy within limits | Decided during the build |
| Best when | You need to be online soon and your enquiries are simple | You are comfortable assembling pages yourself | Your enquiries need dates, usage or project detail |

Choose a template if you are starting out, your work is varied but your enquiries are simple, and you accept that the site will look like others. Choose a builder if you want more control and are willing to put in the time. Choose a custom build if the site has to carry real detail, such as booking dates, licensing terms or project stories, and if a weak enquiry path is costing you work.

If you land on the third route, the work is described on the page about portfolio website design for photographers and filmmakers. To see how working photographers structure their own sites first, look at these photography website examples.

#### Questions about photography website templates

**Question:** Is a template good enough for a photographer who is just starting out?

**Answer:** Often, yes. If you need to be online quickly and your enquiries are simple, a template is a sensible start. Run the ten-minute test first, and keep your images and text in a form you can move later.

**Question:** Can I change a template later?

**Answer:** You can usually change the colours, fonts and content. Changing the structure is harder, and moving to a different template often means rebuilding pages. Plan on moving your content rather than the design.

**Question:** Will a template hurt my search rankings?

**Answer:** Not by itself. Search visibility depends more on your content, how quickly pages load and who links to you than on whether the site began as a template. A template with heavy pages and no text is a problem for those reasons.

**Question:** Should I buy a template or use a website builder?

**Answer:** A template is a fixed design, and a builder lets you rearrange it. If you want to change the structure yourself, choose a builder. If you want the quickest result and are content with the layout, a template is fine.

**Meta description (145 characters):** Why most music studio website templates fail at booking, what a recording studio site needs to show, and a checklist for choosing one that works.

**H1 (62 characters):** Music studio website templates: why booking is where they fail

### Body copy

If you run a recording or rehearsal studio, a template looks like the quick answer. Most of them are built for something else. A music studio website template is often a band site in a different colour, with a player, a list of tour dates and a merchandise strip. A studio has a different job: it has to sell a bookable room.

This guide explains what a recording studio site has to show, why templates tend to fail at booking, and what to look for instead. It does not count down a list of templates. It explains the decision, so you can judge any template yourself.

#### What a recording studio site has to show

Someone deciding whether to book your studio is asking practical questions. They want to see the room before they travel to it. A studio site needs to cover five things.

- **The rooms.** Photographs, dimensions, and what each room suits: tracking a full band, vocals, mixing, podcast recording or rehearsal. If you have several rooms, they should be easy to compare.
- **The equipment.** What is installed, what can be brought in and what is available on request. Keep to what a booker cares about, and skip a catalogue of every cable.
- **Rates or a quote path.** Publish rates if they are stable and simple. If they depend on the project, give a clear “request a quote” route that asks the questions you need answered.
- **Availability.** How a visitor finds out when a room is free, and how far ahead they should ask.
- **House rules.** Arrival and load-in, cancellations, noise and access, and anything a first-time client should know before turning up.

A studio that answers these on the site answers fewer of the same questions by email.

#### Why most studio templates fail at booking

Templates are designed around what most buyers of templates are: bands and solo artists. Their natural content is music to play, shows to attend and merchandise to sell. A studio’s natural content is rooms, dates and specifications. The structure does not match, and the gaps appear in predictable places.

**The layout is about listening, not booking.** The strongest element on the page is usually a player or a video. A booker has to scroll past it to find out whether a room is available.

**Rooms cannot be compared.** Templates tend to present one venue or one act. If you have a live room, a vocal booth and a rehearsal space, you need a way to put them side by side, with specs, photographs and suitable uses.

**The contact form is generic.** A name, an email address and a message box does not separate a session booking from a workshop signup or a collaboration pitch. These are different people who need different replies. A single form leaves you sorting them by hand.

**Booking widgets assume a simple appointment.** Some templates include a calendar or appointment tool. These are often built for fixed-length appointments, not for sessions that involve an engineer, extra hours, equipment requests or a deposit. Check that the tool matches how you actually take bookings.

**There is nowhere for preparation.** A first-time client benefits from knowing what to bring, how long to allow and what to decide beforehand. Templates rarely have a natural place for that, so it ends up in a long email.

#### What to look for instead

If you are choosing a template anyway, test it against this checklist. Open the demo on a phone first.

- Can you give each room its own section with photographs, specs and suitable uses?
- Can you add an equipment list that is easy to scan?
- Is there a clear place for rates, or for a quote request with the right questions?
- Can you show availability or explain how to ask for it?
- Can the form separate session bookings, workshop signups and collaborations?
- Is there room for house rules and preparation, away from the footer?
- Does the first screen help a booker, rather than only showing a player?
- Do the images and any video stay fast on a phone?

A template that meets most of these can work for a small studio with simple needs. If several fail, the site will spend its time looking good while you answer the same questions by hand. In that case the work is closer to what is described under music and art space website design.

For a look at how individual artists structure their own sites, see these artist website examples.

#### Questions about music studio websites

**Question:** Do I need online booking?

**Answer:** Not always. A clear enquiry form that asks for dates, room, session length and what the session involves is enough for many studios. Online booking helps when your availability is simple and your sessions follow a fixed pattern. If sessions vary a lot, a good enquiry path often works better.

**Question:** Should I publish my rates?

**Answer:** If your rates are stable and simple, yes. It saves time for both sides. If they depend on the project, explain what affects the price and give a quote route, so visitors know what to expect.

**Question:** Can a template handle several rooms?

**Answer:** Some can, but many present a single venue. Check whether you can give each room its own page or section, with separate photographs, specs and uses, before you commit.

## 6. /notes/dance-studio-website-templates

**Page type:** Editorial · **Schema:** Article · **Canonical path:** `/notes/dance-studio-website-templates`

**Title tag (full, brand included, 60 characters):** Dance Studio Website Templates: What to Check | iSixDigitals

**Meta description (146 characters):** Dance studio website templates often ship a generic classes page. Here is what to check for timetables, levels and trial class booking on a phone.

**H1 (60 characters):** Dance studio website templates: what to check before you buy

### Body copy

A dance studio website template looks finished in the demo: a hero photograph, a classes section, a team page and a contact form. The problems start when your real timetable goes in and a real parent tries to book a trial class on a phone.

This guide explains what dance studio website templates usually ship, where they fail, and what to check before you commit. It does not rank templates. It gives you a way to test any of them against how a dance studio actually works.

#### What a dance studio template usually ships

Most templates follow the same pattern. There is a home page with a large image, a classes page that lists each class as a card, a gallery, a page for teachers and a contact page with a form. That structure suits many small businesses. A dance studio has a few jobs it does not cover.

A studio needs a timetable that people can actually read, a way to explain levels, a route for trial classes that differs from term registration, and separate paths for parents and adult students. These are the places where templates tend to give out.

#### Where dance studio templates fail

##### The classes page is not a timetable

A list of class cards tells a visitor what you teach. It does not tell them when. If the template includes a timetable block, it is usually a week grid designed for a wide screen. On a phone that grid either shrinks until it cannot be read or scrolls sideways and hides most of the week. A visitor who cannot find a Tuesday evening class in a few seconds will often leave.

A timetable that survives a phone shows one day at a time or a list grouped by day, lets people filter by age group and level, and links each slot to a class page.

##### Levels have nowhere to live

Templates have fields for a title and a description. They rarely have fields for level, age range or what a student needs to be able to do before joining. Studios end up squeezing these into the description, where they are inconsistent and easy to miss. A newcomer then has to phone the front desk to find out whether a class is right for them.

##### One form for every enquiry

A dance studio gets trial bookings, term registrations and general questions. The first needs a class, a date, an age and any experience. The second needs full contact details, emergency contacts, medical notes and consent. The third needs a message box. A single contact form cannot do all three. A form that asks for too much frightens off first-time visitors, and one that asks for too little leaves you chasing details by email.

##### Parents and adults share one path

Templates have a flat navigation: home, classes, about, contact. A parent looking for a children’s class has to scan past adult evening classes and vocational training, and an adult beginner has to scan past the children’s programme. Different audiences need different routes into the same site.

##### The booking tool does not connect

Many studios already use software for registration, payments or class booking. A template may not embed it cleanly, or may force a link to a separate site that looks different. The class page and the booking tool should feel like one journey, with the class already chosen when the visitor arrives.

#### What to check before you choose

Open the demo on a phone, and do these in order.

1. **Put in your real timetable.** Use your actual classes, including the busiest day. See what happens to the layout.
2. **Look at the timetable on a phone.** Can you read a whole day without zooming or scrolling sideways?
3. **Check for filters.** Can a visitor narrow the timetable by age group and level?
4. **Check that each class can open its own page.** That page should hold the level, age range, what to wear, the teacher and the booking route.
5. **Find where levels go.** There should be a proper place for level and age range, not only a free-text description.
6. **Look at the form.** Can you build a short trial form that is separate from the contact form? Can you add a registration route that goes to your existing tool?
7. **Test your booking software.** Find out whether it embeds or links cleanly, and what the visitor sees when they arrive.
8. **Check the navigation.** Can you give children’s, adult and vocational classes their own sections?
9. **Check how you edit the timetable.** If a class changes, how many places do you have to update?
10. **Check page speed on a phone.** Photographs are heavy in dance sites, so see how long it takes before the timetable appears.

A template that meets most of these can serve a small studio with a simple timetable. If it fails several, the timetable will go out of date, the enquiries will arrive without the information you need, and you will end up fixing the site by hand.

#### When a template is enough and when it is not

A template is a sensible choice when you run a small number of classes, your timetable changes rarely, and your enquiries are simple. Be honest about the trade: you accept the template’s structure, and you work around its limits.

A custom build is worth discussing when you have several age groups and programmes, when your timetable changes each term, when trial bookings and registrations need separate handling, or when you want the site to connect properly to your booking tool. That work is described on the page about dance studio website design. For the wider choice between ready-made and built-to-fit, the section on template, builder or custom build lays out the trade-offs, even though it was written for photographers.

#### Questions about dance studio website templates

**Question:** Can a template show a timetable properly?

**Answer:** Some can, if the timetable block adapts to small screens and lets visitors filter by age and level. Many cannot. Put your real timetable into the demo and look at it on a phone before you decide.

**Question:** Can I take trial class bookings with a template?

**Answer:** It depends on the form tools. You need a short trial form that is separate from your general contact form, and ideally one that records the class and date. If the template only supports one contact form, you will need a workaround or a different tool.

**Question:** Does a template save money?

**Answer:** It usually costs less to start, because there is no design stage. The cost shows up later if the timetable needs constant manual updating or if enquiries arrive without the details you need. Weigh the starting cost against the time you will spend running it.
